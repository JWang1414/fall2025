### Capitalocene
In *Cheap Nature* by Raj Patel and Jason Moore.

The Capitalocene is a proposed era in the geological timescale, marked by the results of capitalist civilisation, similar to the Anthropocene. It is a method of acknowledging the alterations humans have applied to Earth, while also highlighting that it is one primarily driven by the need to transform nature into material wealth.

The Capitalocene is one method of identifying the root causes of climate change and modern environmental collapse. it recognises the instability in our use of natural resources, the inefficient strategies we use to terra-form environments, and the consequences resulting from our neglect for the natural world. Working to counteract the dominance of capitalism is analogous to joining the environmental movement. 
### Privatisation of the commons
In *Reclaiming Our Common Home* by Vandana Shiva

"The commons" refer to natural resources required for the sustenance of life, and so should be freely available for everyone. Included in this is water, air, and food. The privatisation of the commons refers to companies laying claim to these common resources, creating artificial scarcity, and profiting off it.

We cannot achieve a sustainable future if man is attempting to dominate and lay claim to natural sources like rivers and plants. The privatisation of the commons requires the destabilisation of the natural order.
### Dualist and hierarchical philosophy of René Descartes
Not just the separation of humans and nature, but the idea that we are better than nature, an ideology presented by Rene Descartes and Francis Bacon. This philosophy strongly supports a anthropocentric worldview, and manipulating nature for our own betterment as we see fit.

Dualist and hierarchical philosophy is the backbone of modern capitalism, and among the grounding justifications used against the environmental movement. "Humans are better than nature, and do not need nature." Despite research suggesting otherwise, and our heavy reliance on natural resources, this ideology permeates all of modern civilisation. Combating this mindset is a requirement for the success of the environmental movement.
### Reclaiming the Commons
In *Reclaiming Our Common Home* by Vandana Shiva

The process of taking the commons back from government or company control, and placing it within the public domain.

To reclaim the commons is both to make essential natural resources freely available, but also to live in conjunction with nature. It comes with the acceptance that we live in tandem with nature, that it provides for us, and we should provide with it. To reclaim the commons is to place power back into nature so that it can thrive, and regenerate.
### Unequal ecologies
A term used by Stephen Sharper to describe how an in-balance of power is embedded within environmental disaster.

Statistically, chemical plants, waste dumps, and other hazardous disposals are more often found and placed within poorer neighbourhoods. This is a deliberate choice made by the wealthy and powerful. Choosing these locations helps to keep the rich in power, and the poor weak and disadvantaged. That is, it maintains the status quo.

Furthermore, the wealth and powerful naturally have more firepower. If the government threatens to construct a power plant inside a rich neighbourhood, people will sue. Inside a poor neighbourhood, they can do nothing. In this sense, it is preying on the weak.

This is a prejudice that prevents us from building just societies. Ideally, this issue must be solved by developing proper waste management practices, and deploying them within cities so nobody is disadvantaged.
### Intersectional environmentalism
The idea presented in *How BLM and Environmental Justice are Connected* by Leah Thomas.

Deeply interconnected to the idea of unequal ecologies. A large majority of poor communities are comprised of minority groups, borne from historical racism. In this sense, unequal ecologies is an expression of racism and oppression, deeply interconnected with other social justice fields.

Again a prejudice that prevents us from building just societies. Equal chances should be given to all people. The environmental movement should recognize this, and join with social justice movements for a common cause.
### Chain of courage
Presented in *Chatting with Stephan Scharper on the Chain of Courage* by Stephan Scharper.

The chain of courage is the idea that one brave act from an individual or movement can incite more people to do the same. The result is a domino effect, or branching chain of people working together towards a common goal, all starting from initially one point.

An example is Rachel Carson's publishing of *Silent Spring*. She started the chain of courage, and scientists, who discreetly delivered studies and research on the topic followed through. The publishing company for *Silent Spring*, and broadcast networks interviewing Rachel Carson on the topic are all part of this chain of courage. Despite facing mass backlash from the chemical industry, each on of them perpetuated a domino effect, inspired from the people in the chain before them.

The chain of courage tells us about our role in the environmental movement. Although you may not be the beginning of the chain, it is regardless important to stand your ground and join as a link in the chain. A movement that may start from just a few brave souls may expand into one that can make real change and betterment.
### Dragons of inaction
A number of psychological barriers people face that limit climate mitigation efforts described by Robert Gifford .

They are very broadly stated to be various forms of: limited cognition, ideologies, comparisons with others, sunk costs, dis-credence, perceived risks, and limited behaviour. They are typically based in the inability to grasp the vast effects of climate change, the inability to push oneself to take steps toward climate mitigation, or misunderstanding the climate movement.

Interestingly, Gifford brings up people's tendency to counteract their own climate efforts. That is, if somebody finds a strategy to reduce their emissions, they may be more likely to repeat this strategy more often, offsetting the benefit.

To build a sustainable society, we need everybody on board, so we need to know how these barriers manifest, and how we can help people move past them. Understanding the dragons of inaction should, ideally, improve science communication, and help establish beneficial climate policies in the future.
### What was once unimaginable became mainstream
From Rebecca Solnit in *Not Too Late*

It is based on the idea that "what is difficult is not the same as impossible". In the past, we have seen countless circumstances where challenging or difficult ideas have come to fruition. Social change that might have been thought impossible eventually becoming so developed and integrated into society it is part of the everyday.

An example is the Ontario Clean Air Alliance. Their goal was to shut down all coal fired plants in Ontario. Despite how absurd this claim might have sounded, it was achieved in 2014, and is now normalised within Ontario.

It helps to suggest we may be too cynical. Regardless of how hopeless it might look, there is always the possibility for improvement. To work towards a better future, it is better to hope we succeed instead of succumbing to pessimism.
### Thought experiment of Planet A or B
From *Humankind: A Hopeful History* by Rutger Bregman.

A thought experiment that details the following: If a plane is about to crash, do we live on planet A or B? On planet A, people are calm, collected, help themselves and then help whoever is beside them. On planet B, everybody panics, activity on the plane devolves into fighting, pushing, every man for themselves.

Although a majority of people respond that we live on planet B, an overwhelming portion of studies suggest that we live on planet A. It demonstrates that humans have a far more pessimistic view of ourselves than reality.

Ultimately, this thought experiment encourages people to reconsider the values the average person holds. The modern perception of humanity is that people are innately selfish. The fact that this is incorrect implies that the systems we have established to "control" this greed are ill-conceived, and so the groundwork of our societies is faulty.

It means that people are more than willing to work together for the benefit of everyone. The possibility of a worldwide environmental community is more likely than you might think.
