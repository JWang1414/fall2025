# Gaia as a science 
Largely researched by independent English chemist James Lovelock

The theory that organisms and their environment evolve as a single, self-regulating system. This system manifests as a complex series of positive and negative feedback loops that maintains a constant balance in the ecosystem even if there are large scale changes in the climate.

Reading example: The weathering of calcium silicate rock helps to remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. When it is warmer, rock weathering increases due to rainfall and organisms in the rock, resulting in a negative feedback loop, regulating the temperature.
- Rainfall increases in warmer weather
- The growth of micro-organisms on the rocks increases in warmer weather

- Scientific backing that the ecosystem is deeply connected, on a global scale
	- Supports Leopold's land ethic
- Evolution theory is dominated by the idea that ecosystems are purely competitive. Gaia theory proves that the biosphere is deeply competitive and cooperative
	- Connected to gift economics. Shows us that successful economies need not be purely competitive. In fact, the complex systems that regular Earth are often cooperative.
- Provides a biological basis for environmental ethics. If Earth is a living system that self-regulates, it is harder to justify treating it as inert matter
# Gaia as a metaphor 
Largely researched by independent English chemist James Lovelock

The theory that organisms and their environment evolve as a single, self-regulating system. This system manifests as a complex series of positive and negative feedback loops that maintains a constant balance in the ecosystem even if there are large scale changes in the climate.

The metaphorical name helps to communicate scientific ideas in a way that is easier to grasp. Public support for the theory was massively bolstered as a result. This wide acceptance empowers people to adopt a mindset that re-frames humans as part of an integrated whole.

- Changes in the public mindset can have huge consequences. Environmental education can improve. More people help push policy changes. Greater support for environmental studies.
- To accept Gaia theory is to accept you are a part of the Earth. It helps to develop a love for the Earth, a love often considered prerequisite to large scale climate improvement
	- The interconnection suggested by Gaia theory, trophic cascades, and many other similar theories also shows us that our participation in environmental activism may result in an "invisible improvement" that may not be immediately obvious.
# McGregor’s M&M-Macaroon metaphor 
Described by Deborah McGregor in 2020.

The M&M and Macaroon metaphor is a method of describing the different interpretations of humanity's location in the biosphere. In an M&M, the peanut and shell make up the full M&M, but they are separate from each other. In a macaroon, the coconut and chocolate are built into each other, they are inseparable, you have to destroy the macaroon to separate the two.

McGregor critiques the common separation between humans and nature, using the M&M to illustrate this divide, and contrasts it with the Indigenous perspective, which she likens to the macaroon.

- Current legal systems often fail to reflect the Indigenous understanding conveyed by the macaroon that humans are part of nature
	- Including a debate as to whether air should be included as a part of the environment
- Anishinaabek legal tradition is based on obligations and responsibilities to the earth, rather than rights to exploit it
	- Polluting lakes, and blocking rivers infringes on water's duty to provide life.
- McGregor is providing an alternative method of developing the rights and responsibilities of humans, while also maintaining the fact that humans are not the centrepiece of the ecosystem
	- Even better, we have all of Indigenous history to use as the groundwork
# Leopold’s Land Ethic 
Stated by American conservationist Aldo Leopold, who lived from 1887-1948

> "A thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community. It is wrong when it tends otherwise."

Among the guiding principles of this course. Leopold suggests "thinking like a mountain", and understanding the dynamics of life from the perspective of a common member in the biotic community
- Challenges the idea that ethics should concern exclusively human interests. Ethics should be expanded to other living beings, and yes, even the land.
- Shifts our idea of land as economic property to biological community.

Leopold tells us that, to foster an obligation towards the Earth, we must instill "love, respect, and admiration" for the land. It is not enough to just educate people about ecology, but we also must give them proper motivation and reason to support the environmental community.

- Despite human hubris, we do not have a strong grasp on the workings of the Earth, and so we cannot assume the role as the conquerors.
- The land ethic offers a straightforward and practical ethical framework. It is a simple, and concrete way to evaluate decisions and advocate for policies.
# Trophic cascades 
Term coined by American zoologist Robert Paine

An ecological phenomenon triggered by the addition or removal of top predators. Often results in dramatic changes in the ecosystem structure and nutrient cycling due to the effect it has on prey populations.

Class example: Wolves added back to Yellowstone, curbing the deer population, and therefore restoring the wildlife they were overeating. This cascade continues until even the rivers benefit from the wolves.

- Scientific backing for the importance of every piece of the ecosystem
	- Supports Leopold's land ethic, and so encourages the same mindset
- The biosphere is constantly maintained in a delicate balance, even slight disturbances like this can result in dramatic effects
- Humans, who often have a weak grasp of the complexity of the biosphere, should not see themselves as the conquerors and owners
	- Think like a mountain. How can we justify adjusting the ecosystem to our own benefits, when it would disadvantage the mountain? We live on it.
# Decoupling and the rebound effect 
Presented in *Questioning Economic Growth* in 2010 by Peter Victor.

Victor is criticizing the popular method of "green growth". Green growth is the process of decoupling economic growth from material and energy costs. It is essentially striving for growth while attempting to reduce the impacts on the planet.

The rebound effect states that, as something grows less costly and cheaper to use, the use of it will increase. This increase in use is often enough to increase the sum costs.

As an example: despite the best steam engines in 1910 being roughly x36 times more efficient than those in 1760, there was a 2000-fold rise in steam-power use.

- Tells us that green growth is not viable. The net damage done on the environment will increase with growth, irrespective of how environmentally efficient our technology becomes
- Decreasing economic growth looks more promising. It is not just better for the environment, but more sustainable for humans.
- Focus in economic growth also appears to increasing inequality. The wage gap continues to increase. And, under this regime, developed countries are encouraged to continue encroaching on developing countries.
# The role of gratitude and reciprocity within an economic system 
Discussed in *The Serviceberry: An Economy of Abundance* in 2022 by Robin Wall Kimmerer.

Kimmerer contrasts two economic systems: market economies built on scarcity and gift economies based on abundance and reciprocity. In gift economies, wealth and surplus is shared within the community, as opposed to hoarded (creating artificial scarcity), like in market economies.

Reading example: Hunter who stores meat "in the belly of my brother" by hosting a feast rather than hoarding his surplus. The serviceberry produces abundant fruit to feed birds, who in turn distribute seeds and ensure the tree's reproduction.
- The stability of the gift economy lies in the use of gratitude as a resource. It is something Kimmerer says isn't just renewable, but increases with every gesture of kindness.

- Capitalist systems privatize freely available gifts from Earth, like water, to create profit
	- Gift economies build a strong community and mutual flourishing
- Although market capitalism is unlikely to be replaced, constructing local gift economies doesn't just suggest the possibility of something different, but helps us hope for a vast and sustainable economy.
# Psychological resilience 
Discussed in two articles written by Simon Appolloni, in 2022 and 2023.

Define as the ability to cope mentally and emotionally with crisis. Psychologically resilient people typically return to pre-crisis status quicker. In terms of the climate crisis, this implies somebody who understands not just the issues in the world, but also how things are improving. This strategy (known as dialectical thinking) fosters a sort of hope that empowers action.

- Teaching students psychological resilience is proven to reduce "eco-anxiety" and "eco-paralysis". This is healthier, and increases the number of people taking climate action.
- Dialectical thinking promotes a more factual view of the world, without simplifying things into black and white.
	- A dialectical thinker knows what needs to be done, and what is currently being done.
